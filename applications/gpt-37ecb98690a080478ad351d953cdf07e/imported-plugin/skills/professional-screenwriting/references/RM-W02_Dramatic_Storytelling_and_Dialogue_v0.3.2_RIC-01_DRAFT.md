# RM-W02 — Dramatic Storytelling & Dialogue
## Operational reference for professional screenwriting | v0.3.2 RIC-01 DRAFT

**Status:** KR-01 revised knowledge DRAFT; revision work owner-authorized but content awaits approval for Plugin use. **Focus:** contrasting voices, visual/non-linear examples, reveal fairness, language and continuity checks.  
**Consultation (RIC-01 clarification):** For professional **narrative** screenplay drafting and relevant critique, consult this reference for applicable **visual-scene causality, scene turn, reveal scheduling, or dialogue craft**. A silent dramatic scene can require its *visual-scene / reveal* guidance even when `DIALOGUE = NOT_APPLICABLE`. Do not request spoken lines, score dialogue, or claim dialogue defects in a truly silent scene. A purely abstract/poetic **non-narrative** work with none of these dimensions may legitimately skip RM-W02 with a recorded reason. RM-W02 is not a universal Quick Storyboard requirement and does not supersede RM-C07 format rules.  
**Authority:** Advisory craft standards, distinct from existing RM-C07 dialogue-output format and RM-C01 cinematic visual rhythm. No copied copyrighted screenplay dialogue or external script excerpts.

## 1. Principle: dramatize change, do not merely announce meaning

Drama becomes engaging when *choices have consequences* and the audience can follow why a character acts. Emotional engagement is not equal to loud conflict, sensational revelation, fast editing or manipulative mystery. A compelling line of dialogue often matters because it changes a relationship, withholds something responsibly, reveals character, or prompts action.

Use professional storytelling tools to serve the author's purpose. Bible fidelity and project-level moral constraints remain hard limits; do not introduce fabricated words to Scripture for an exciting hook.

## 2. The microstructure of a scene

| Element | Diagnostic question | Common weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Why are we here now? What can the audience infer? | Aimless setup |
| Objective | What does at least one character want in this encounter? | Nobody drives the exchange |
| Obstacle | Why is the goal difficult? | Dialogue merely confirms what we know |
| Tactic | What changes in how the character attempts to get it? | Same line repeated louder |
| Turn | What changes—knowledge, power, options, connection? | Scene ends where it started |
| Exit | What must happen next because of this change? | Arbitrary transition |

A contemplative or visual scene may turn through a look, gesture, motif or realization without explicit dialogue. Do not prescribe shouting or conflict to such scenes.

## 3. Subtext and character voice

**Text** is what is said. **Subtext** is what the person means, fears, avoids or seeks, inferred from context and behavior. Effective subtext need not be cryptic: it should remain intelligible when paired with action, prior setup or reaction.

Differentiate voices by:
- relationship to the listener and relative power;
- patience, directness, rhythm, vocabulary, metaphor and hesitation;
- professional/social role **without** caricaturing identity;
- what each character knows, conceals, wants and refuses to say;
- how they speak under pressure compared with safety.

Avoid identical polished speeches for every character. Avoid inserting a lesson directly into every character's mouth. Preserve the user's desired tone, locale and language; do not impose a particular dialect.

## 4. Dialogue diagnostic checklist

Evaluate each exchange for:

1. **Purpose:** Is the line doing character or plot work?
2. **Necessity:** Can visual action carry this information better?
3. **Authenticity:** Would *this* character say it in *this* moment?
4. **Tension:** Are there competing intentions or emotional stakes?
5. **Exposition:** Is the speaker explaining something the other party already knows solely for the viewer?
6. **Listen/reaction:** Does the other character respond in a way that changes the conversation?
7. **Silence:** Might an interruption, action or pause be more expressive than an explanation?
8. **Time:** Can these lines plausibly fit the estimated scene duration?
9. **Attribution:** Is each line unambiguously spoken by a specific character and scene?
10. **Source honesty:** Are Bible quotations exact and sourced, and invented lines labeled?

“More natural” must never become a justification for changing an approved fact or moral claim.

## 5. Original example: exposition vs conflict

**Setup:** An apprentice has broken a rare watch piece. Her mentor asks why the hand is stuck.

**Over-explained draft:**

> APPRENTICE: As you know, you trained me for three years, and tomorrow we must deliver the heirloom to Ms. Wu, but I accidentally damaged its gear yesterday when I worked alone.
>
> MENTOR: I understand, and honesty has always been important in our workshop.

**Revised dramatic possibility:**

> MENTOR: It stopped at 4:17 again.
>
> APPRENTICE: The replacement gear is in. You can sign the receipt.
>
> MENTOR: That's not what I asked.
>
> (The apprentice turns the watch face down. The mentor notices the scratch on her thumb.)
>
> APPRENTICE: I was going to tell you after the delivery.

**Why it may be stronger:** visible evidence replaces backstory recitation; distinct priorities create tension; the apprentice makes a choice. **Tradeoff:** details of the client's schedule may need to be established elsewhere. This is one option, not an absolute rewrite, and should be compared against the intended audience/tone.

## 5A. Character-voice fingerprint and contrast test (KR-01)

For dialogue-heavy scripts, describe a compact `voice_fingerprint`: vocabulary/register, typical clause length, directness, avoidance habits, how the speaker listens, power position and change under stress. Treat it as an **editable craft description**, never as a stereotype, fixed accent or diagnostic label. Test by swapping speaker names on 3–5 lines: if identity remains indistinguishable without a dramatic reason, explore differences in goals and behavior, not cosmetic slang.

**Original same-information contrast:** The store has one day to fix the leak. A direct caretaker might say, “The ceiling is wet. Call the landlord now.” A conflict-avoidant caretaker might say, “Could we ask whether the upstairs pipe was checked?” Both respond to the *same* observed evidence. Neither speech pattern must be forced across every line; power and stakes can change the style. During a rewrite, compare old/new `voice_fingerprint` and require intentional, story-grounded change.

## 5B. Three genre-diverse demonstrations (KR-01; original examples)

**A. Silent / visual scene** — A woman returns a borrowed umbrella to an empty reception desk; she finds an old receipt showing the desk clerk repaired it for her. She leaves a folded thank-you note but, hearing approaching footsteps, retrieves the note and waits in person. **Scene turn:** from avoidance to a choice for encounter. There is no compulsory dialogue, reveal shock or fast cut. Diagnose visual causality by tracking objects/choices; check that the receipt is legible and legitimately sourced in-story.

**B. Two-character high-pressure dialogue** — In a school workshop, a teen needs the team's robot returned before judging; their partner is withholding the battery after discovering an unsafe connection.

TEEN: “They're calling our number.”
PARTNER: “It overheated yesterday.”
TEEN: “Then tell me what will make you hand it over.”
PARTNER (unplugs battery): “A test without the cover on.”

**Scene turn:** argument becomes a concrete safety test; both characters have legitimate goals. **Failure variant:** replacing the last line with “You never care about me!” shifts the scene to generic accusation and may destroy the causal safety dilemma. **Voice check:** does the teenager's urgency and partner's evidence-based reluctance persist across surrounding scenes?

**C. Contemplative / non-linear image sequence** — A child draws the same tree in three seasons, intercut with an older adult finding those sketches. The final drawing contains two small figures where earlier pages show one. This can be meaningful without declaring the connection in dialogue. **Check:** time-order cues, whether the drawings could plausibly exist, and whether viewers need an anchoring shot. The emotional result is a creative hypothesis, not measured engagement.

These examples illustrate different mechanisms; they are **not templates** requiring all stories to introduce a twist or speaking character.

## 8A. Reveal fairness and information schedule (KR-01)

For scripts with an intentional reveal, record `setup_or_seed_anchor`, `reveal_anchor`, `character_knowledge_before/after`, `audience_knowledge_before/after`, `retrospective_consistency`, and unresolved disclosure risks. The viewer does not need enough information to guess the twist; however, the film may not make an impossible earlier fact true merely to surprise them. If the work has **no** reveal, mark this check `NOT_APPLICABLE`—do not invent one.

**Example:** SC01 establishes a delivery on Friday; SC03 states that the **mutually agreed contractual pickup date is Tuesday**, contradicting SC01's established Friday appointment **unless** the screenplay shows the agreement being changed. A revision that creates this issue must be referred to RM-W03 for cross-scene regression analysis even if it increases tension.

## 6. Hook and curiosity—without manufacturing deception

A beginning should establish meaningful orientation and a reason to care. Legitimate hooks include:
- a person making a difficult choice;
- an unusual contradiction the viewer can understand;
- a promise or goal threatened by immediate obstacle;
- an emotionally consequential encounter;
- a visual image charged with meaning.

Do not make a loud reveal, cliffhanger or question mandatory. Quiet stories can earn engagement through specificity, empathy and beauty. Do not promise “red-fruit style retention” or specific audience metrics without evidence.

## 7. Escalation and emotional rhythm

Escalation can be tighter options, changed relationships, increased moral cost, new information, competing obligations, or forced choices. Track **state change**, not just sound level or shot frequency. A useful tension map describes how the character's available choices narrow or widen, and where the viewer needs rest to absorb meaning.

For a short film, too many reversals often weaken credibility. Use fewer, better-developed developments. The final moment should be a logical and emotional consequence, not merely the writer's preferred sermon.

**Pacing vs camera rhythm:** narrative pacing controls information, decisions, pressure and emotional time; RM-C01 addresses later audiovisual shot/movement rhythm. Consult RM-C01 for cinematic design, not as a substitute for scene causality.

## 8. Foreshadowing, reveals, reversals

- **Setup/payoff:** Establish a meaningful fact, behavior or image that later gains consequence.
- **Foreshadowing:** Provide a fair but not necessarily obvious cue; does not require the audience to predict.
- **Reveal:** New information changes the audience's interpretation.
- **Reversal:** A meaningful change in expected direction, power or outcome.

Check reveal legitimacy: Does the character know? Could viewers reasonably have had access to relevant hints? Is the revelation compatible with continuity and source facts? Does the reveal generate new meaningful choice? Avoid manipulating disability, suffering, ethnicity, poverty or faith as a cheap “twist” without care and credibility.

## 9. Emotional payoff and endings

An ending feels earned when it follows choices, establishes meaning, and honors the opening promise. Forms include resolution, bittersweet acceptance, open but complete emotional decision, tragic recognition, or contemplative image. An **open ending** can be complete; an **unanswered plot** is not automatically artistic.

Questions for the writer: What changes externally? What changes internally or relationally? What evidence on screen supports that change? Does the audience need a speech to understand? Do not compel a happy ending against source truth or story tone.

## 10. Script formatting and filmability

Use readable production-oriented conventions, without asserting exact legal/contest format. Preserve requested dialect/language in character speech. For Traditional Chinese dialogue, check that characters sound conversational rather than all speaking literary exposition; verify punctuation and speaker attribution; if subtitles are a deliverable, flag long rapid speeches, overlapping speech, off-screen speech and pauses for downstream timing/legibility review. Do **not** assume a fixed characters-per-second limit without actual voice/subtitle requirements:

```
SCENE SC03 | INT. WATCH SHOP — EVENING | est. 35–50 seconds
Purpose: The apprentice's concealment becomes unsustainable.
Required continuity: case lid scratched; bronze watch; evening light.

The mentor places the silent watch on the wooden counter.
The apprentice slides a receipt toward him, avoiding eye contact.

MENTOR
It stopped at 4:17 again.

APPRENTICE
The replacement gear is in. You can sign.

The mentor turns the watch face down. The apprentice keeps her hand flat
against the counter to hide a fresh scratch.
```

Each scene should identify scene ID, location/time when meaningful, visible action, speaker/cue, dialogue, optional performance note, estimated duration and non-negotiable source/prop facts. Avoid overly dense cinematography in the screenplay: use the existing Director cinematic workflow for shot design. Format for screen readability first; a downstream target may need a different script syntax.

## 11. Scoped revision operations

When user requests “make this dialogue stronger,” do not rewrite the entire movie by default. Process:

1. Pin `screenplay_id`, revision, scene IDs, exact span and preserved facts.
2. Identify the specific dramatic task the dialogue should accomplish.
3. Diagnose evidence and classify if there is no weakness; do not invent a defect.
4. Offer 1–3 differentiated alternatives where choice matters (e.g., restrained, confrontational, subtle).
5. Explain what each choice improves and risks.
6. Await selected option for consequential changes; never overwrite before approval.
7. Apply minimal delta, update revision/ledger; recheck character/plot/voice continuity. Explicitly hand off impacted preceding/following scenes, who-knows-what, prop/time facts, reveal seeds and duration estimates to RM-W03 regression checks.
8. Make source and Bible quotation handling explicit.

## 12. Safe adaptation types

| Work mode | How to write | Source boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Original fiction | Creative invention allowed within user constraints | Mark AI-proposed vs owner-specified |
| Inspirational story | Prefer values emerging from choices, not slogans | Existing RM-S03/S04 governs values evaluation |
| Biblical story | Preserve canonical events; flag imaginative staging | Scripture wording and meaning supersede style |
| Narrative music video | Can use visual beats, motifs and poetic scenes | Traditional dialogue/three-act structure optional |
| Critique-only import | Diagnose, do not rewrite without scope approval | Imported ownership/quotation rights remain unresolved unless supplied |

## 13. Failure modes and remedies

- **All characters sound alike:** differentiate listening, diction, knowledge, desires and relationship, not arbitrary accents.
- **Dialogue explains theme too early:** replace speech with consequential behavior or delayed reveal, if source permits.
- **False urgency:** restore real relational/ethical stakes rather than invented clocks.
- **Plot twist rewrites earlier facts:** revise setup or remove twist; do not conceal contradiction.
- **Scene too talkative for runtime:** estimate read-aloud and performance pauses, condense redundant lines, mark timing uncertain.
- **Artificial sentimentality:** allow restraint, ambiguity, or credible cost.
- **Bible quotation invented:** remove claimed quote, check user-supplied passage, relabel imaginative lines.

## 14. Output and consultation evidence

Required screenplay output on professional path: scene/beat IDs and purpose, filmable action, dialogue/voice cues as applicable, duration estimates, narrative/source constraints and explicit draft status. Existing RM-C07 may govern additional dialogue/narration cue formatting, but RM-W02 supplies the **dramatic content** logic. When Critic evaluates revisions, consult RM-W03 as well; document what was consulted and any source that could not be accessed. No automatic assertion that improved prose equals a stronger film.

## KR-01 integration and boundaries

For dialogue-related suggestions, RM-W02 governs **dramatic content and intelligibility**; existing RM-C07 governs **dialogue/narration/performance cue output** when invoked. A script scene may map to several storyboard shots; RM-C01 controls audiovisual shot rhythm later, not plot beats. For scripted dialogue revisions and material cross-scene effects, apply RM-W03's evidence, owner decision, and regression controls. Report reference ID/revision and actual knowledge consulted; no automatic audience-retention claim.
